Pulmonary Sequestration: Symptoms, Diagnosis and Treatment

A guide to the congenital lung malformation in which a segment of lung tissue has no normal airway connection and receives blood from the aorta, and how it is recognised and treated, for patients seen at Apollo Hospitals, Financial District, Hyderabad.

What is pulmonary sequestration?

Pulmonary sequestration is a rare congenital malformation, present from birth, in which a piece of lung tissue develops separately from the normal airways. Because it is not connected to the bronchial tree in the usual way, it does not take part in breathing. It also has an unusual blood supply: instead of the pulmonary artery, it is fed by a branch arising directly from the aorta or one of its branches.

There are two main types:

  • Intralobar sequestration - the more common type, lying within a normal lobe and sharing its pleural covering. It usually drains back into the pulmonary veins and most often affects the lower lobes, especially on the left. It typically presents in older children or adults with repeated infections.
  • Extralobar sequestration - a separate mass with its own pleural covering, sometimes below the diaphragm. It drains into the systemic veins, is often detected on antenatal ultrasound or in infancy, and is more often associated with other congenital anomalies such as congenital diaphragmatic hernia.

Symptoms

Symptoms depend on the type and age at diagnosis. Some sequestrations cause no symptoms and are found by chance. When symptoms occur, they include:

  • Recurrent pneumonia in the same part of the lung - the most typical presentation of intralobar sequestration in adolescents and adults.
  • Persistent cough with sputum - sometimes with fever and weight loss when the sequestration becomes chronically infected or forms an abscess.
  • Coughing up blood - which can occasionally be heavy because of the high-pressure systemic artery.
  • Chest pain or breathlessness - particularly with infection.
  • Breathing or feeding difficulty in newborns - with large extralobar lesions, or rarely heart strain from high blood flow through the abnormal vessels.

Causes

Sequestration arises during early development of the lung in the womb, when an extra bud of lung tissue forms from the primitive foregut and keeps its early blood supply from the aorta. It is not caused by anything the mother did during pregnancy and is not usually inherited. Some experts believe that a proportion of intralobar sequestrations in adults are acquired after chronic infection, but the congenital explanation applies to most cases.

In India, a sequestration presenting as a recurrent lower-lobe shadow with cough and weight loss is sometimes treated as tuberculosis before the true diagnosis is made. A pneumonia that keeps returning to exactly the same place should always prompt a search for an underlying structural cause.

How pulmonary sequestration is diagnosed

The key to diagnosis is showing the abnormal artery from the aorta.

TestWhat it shows
Antenatal ultrasoundA bright lung mass with a feeding vessel from the aorta, often seen before birth
Chest X-rayA persistent opacity or cystic area, usually at a lung base
CT angiography of the chestThe systemic feeding artery, the venous drainage and the structure of the abnormal tissue - the main diagnostic test
MRI or MR angiographyAn alternative without radiation, useful in children
BronchoscopyUsed to exclude an obstructing lesion or foreign body when the diagnosis is uncertain

Needle biopsy is generally avoided, because the abnormal vessels can bleed. Other conditions that can look similar include congenital pulmonary airway malformation, lung abscess, bronchiectasis, a pulmonary arteriovenous malformation and lung cancer.

Treatment

Surgery

Surgical removal is the standard treatment for symptomatic sequestration and is generally advised for intralobar sequestration even when symptoms are mild, because infection tends to recur and can become severe. For intralobar lesions this usually means removing the affected lobe or segment; extralobar lesions can often be removed on their own. Most operations are now performed by video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery, which means smaller incisions and quicker recovery. The abnormal artery must be identified and secured carefully, and planning is done with a thoracic surgeon using the CT angiogram.

Endovascular treatment

Blocking the feeding artery with coils or plugs through a catheter (embolisation) can control bleeding and is sometimes used alone, particularly in small children or in patients who are not fit for surgery, or before surgery to reduce bleeding.

Treatment of infection

Active infection is treated with antibiotics before planned surgery wherever possible.

Children and antenatally detected lesions

Children are managed jointly with paediatric pulmonology and paediatric surgery. Some small extralobar sequestrations without symptoms are observed, and some shrink over time; the decision between surgery and monitoring is individual.

Outlook and follow-up

The outlook after complete removal is excellent. Recurrent infections stop, and the remaining lung compensates fully for the removed segment or lobe, so most people return to normal activity, including sport. A follow-up visit with a chest X-ray is arranged after surgery. Patients who are being observed rather than operated on need regular review and should report any chest infection promptly.

When to seek urgent care

Seek emergency care for coughing up more than a small amount of blood, high fever with breathlessness, chest pain, or breathlessness at rest. In infants, rapid breathing, poor feeding, bluish colour or difficulty breathing need immediate assessment.

Frequently asked questions

Is pulmonary sequestration a cancer?

No. It is a congenital malformation, not a tumour. However, it can look like a mass on scans, which is why CT angiography is important to confirm the diagnosis.

Does every sequestration need surgery?

Surgery is generally advised for intralobar sequestration and for any sequestration that causes symptoms. Some small extralobar sequestrations found before birth or in infancy without symptoms are monitored instead.

Will I have less lung function after the operation?

The sequestered tissue does not contribute to breathing, and the remaining lung compensates well after removal of a segment or lobe. Most people notice no limitation afterwards.

Why was I treated for tuberculosis first?

A sequestration can cause cough, fever, weight loss and a persistent shadow on the X-ray, which can resemble tuberculosis. A shadow that keeps returning in the same place despite treatment should prompt a CT scan with contrast to look for a structural cause.

My baby's antenatal scan showed a lung mass. What happens next?

Many such lesions are small and cause no problems at birth. Your baby will be assessed after delivery, usually with imaging, and care is planned jointly by paediatric pulmonology and paediatric surgery.

Can sequestration come back after surgery?

When the abnormal tissue and its feeding artery are completely removed, the condition does not come back.
